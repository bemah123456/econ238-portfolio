Ballot Question

Shall the United States create a $10 billion, ten-year Flood Buyout Fund that, through state agencies modeled on New Jersey's Blue Acres program, offers every owner of a FEMA-designated Severe Repetitive Loss home, within three years, a voluntary purchase at pre-flood fair market value plus relocation assistance; demolishes purchased homes; permanently restricts that land to floodplain habitat and public open space; and publishes yearly counts of offers made, homes bought, and acres restored?

What's the deal? 141 words

  • What we buyFewer families in harm's way, and thousands of acres of floodplain that soak up water instead of pushing it into the next neighborhood. About 44,600 "Severe Repetitive Loss" homes have flooded roughly five times each. They are about 0.6% of insured homes but drew about 10% of federal flood-insurance claims.
  • What we give upHouses, neighbors, and local property-tax base.
  • Who benefitsOwners who want out, flood-insurance policyholders (the program owes the Treasury $22.5 billion), taxpayers who fund disaster aid, downstream towns, and wildlife.
  • Who bears the costsTowns losing tax revenue, neighbors who stay, and renters who must move.
  • Who paysFederal taxpayers, $1 billion a year for ten years, about $3 per American per year.
  • ScaleAt roughly $250,000 per home (purchase, demolition, restoration, relocation help), $10 billion covers about 40,000 homes and roughly 8,000 acres.

The best case for YES 146 words

This is the rare climate policy that pays for itself. FEMA-funded buyouts of river-flood homes return about $7 in avoided losses for every $1 spent, according to the National Institute of Building Sciences.

Right now we pay to rebuild the same houses over and over. One $69,000 Mississippi home flooded 34 times and collected $663,000 in claims. That isn't insurance. It's a subsidy for staying in harm's way.

Nobody is forced to move. Owners who say yes get pre-flood value and a fresh start; owners who say no keep their homes. New Jersey shows it works: Blue Acres has bought about 1,200 homes and closes deals in months, not the federal median of five-plus years.

The land doesn't sit idle. It becomes wetland, park, and flood storage that protects the homes left standing. And every year we wait, the list of repeatedly flooded homes gets longer.

The best case for NO 148 words

Buyouts are a slow, expensive way to remove a sliver of the risk. Ten billion dollars buys at most about 40,000 homes, while more than 250,000 properties have filed repeat flood claims and climate change keeps adding more.

Voluntary programs also leave checkerboards. If only some neighbors sell, the town still plows the roads, runs the sewers, and sends fire trucks to the holdouts, now with a smaller tax base. Research in Houston found buyout lots tend to pull down nearby home values.

Paying pre-flood value can reward people for building or buying in a floodplain, while renters, who own nothing, get little.

Cheaper tools exist: elevating homes, charging honest risk-based insurance premiums, and banning new construction in floodplains.

Finally, federal money is unreliable. FEMA tried to end its main pre-disaster mitigation grant program in 2025. A promise that may never be funded can leave families in limbo.

Where is the body buried? 96 words

The 7-to-1 return is an average across past projects, not a promise for these homes. Uptake is the biggest unknown: if only a third of owners accept, we get checkerboards, not restored floodplains. "Pre-flood fair market value" for a cheap, flood-damaged house often won't buy a comparable home somewhere safe, so families may move far away or into worse housing. About 20% of these homes sit outside FEMA's mapped flood zones, so the maps guiding the program are themselves wrong. And the people with the least say, renters and elderly long-time residents, may lose the most.

Why isn't this already happening? 132 words

Concentrated costs, diffuse benefits.
A town that loses 50 homes feels the tax hit at once; the savings land on a national insurance program and federal taxpayers years later. Local officials decide but don't capture the gains.
Money arrives after disasters.
Most federal buyout funds flow through post-disaster grants, so the process starts when people are traumatized and takes a median of five-plus years. Many rebuild or sell to speculators first.
Place attachment.
Owners value their homes above market price, and a voluntary program can't guarantee the whole-block buyouts that make restoration work.
Politics rewards rebuilding.
A rebuilt street makes a ribbon-cutting; an empty lot doesn't. Subsidized insurance has long muted the signal to move.
Capacity.
Wealthier counties have staff to apply, so FEMA buyouts cluster there, not where risk is greatest.
